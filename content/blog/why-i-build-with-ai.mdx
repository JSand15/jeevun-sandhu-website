A question I get a lot: _if you're not writing the code yourself, are you really building it?_

I think that question assumes there's one legit way to build software, and I don't think that's true anymore.

## What I actually do

I scope the product, make the design and architecture calls, and direct an AI engineering tool (Claude Code) to actually build it. I review what it makes, push back when it's wrong, and keep going until it's right. That's a real skill. It's just a different one than typing every line by hand.

Here's roughly what that loop looks like for a new feature:

```txt
1. Define the problem and the smallest version that solves it
2. Direct the implementation
3. Use the product myself, break it on purpose
4. Fix what's broken, cut what's not needed
5. Ship
```

## Why this matters more than it used to

A few years ago, this wouldn't have worked. Directing an AI to build real software needed either the AI to be way better than it was, or the person directing it to already know how to code well enough to catch every mistake. Neither one was true yet.

That's changed. Not because building got easier, it didn't, but because the bottleneck moved somewhere else. Now the bottleneck is judgment. Knowing what's worth building, being able to tell if something is actually good, and being stubborn enough to keep going instead of settling for the first version that works.

> The parts of building that used to take years of engineering experience are way more accessible now. The parts that take taste, judgment, and persistence aren't, and never will be.

## What I'm not claiming

I'm not claiming to be a senior engineer. I'm not saying this approach beats traditional engineering for every team or every problem. What I am saying is that for a 15-year-old with no funding and no team, this is the difference between just having an idea and having a product that people actually use.

That's enough for me to keep doing it.
